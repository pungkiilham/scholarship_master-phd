## The Essay (~500 words)

Over eight years as an IT Project Manager I have learned that delivery depends on relationships, not plans. My goal is to become a dosen who bridges industry and academia in Indonesia, and the Chevening alumni network is the platform I will use to scale that mission.

My current role at Diginergy GmbH, a German renewable-energy software firm where I manage projects remotely from Indonesia, is a daily exercise in building relationships across distance and culture. I began by earning one-on-one trust with each German client and developer — learning their pain points, communication styles, and constraints before proposing anything. By translating client requirements into technical tasks developers genuinely understood, and by establishing a "Squad Culture" of shared ownership, we launched an Odoo-based SaaS product in a four-week cycle and cut project timelines by 20%. The lesson I carry forward: reciprocity. I offer value before I ask for it, maintain relationships through honest updates and structured retrospectives, and stay engaged long after a project closes.

Earlier, at Estro Hutama Indonesia, I did the same for industrial manufacturers. I ran workshops to decompose complex stakeholder needs into Business Requirement Documents and technical tasks, and conducted operational audits that identified bottlenecks — raising client ROI and project capacity by 20%. Those relationships still open doors; former clients refer new work and trust my recommendations on technology roadmaps. I keep a simple discipline: celebrate my network's wins, share useful knowledge without being asked, and follow up deliberately rather than opportunistically.

I am applying to Chevening not only for the degree but for a network of alumni who embody the value I want to live: returning home to build. As a future dosen in Engineering Management, I plan to activate the Chevening network in three concrete ways. First, during my year in the UK I will join the Indonesian Chevening societies and volunteer in Chevening's alumni mentoring programme, co-organising career panels for young engineers. Second, on returning to Surabaya I will partner with Indonesian Chevening alumni to co-deliver workshops that close the industry–academia gap, teaching engineering management skills to students and SMEs — aligned with the UK's sustainable-development priorities in Indonesia. Third, I will keep the pipeline alive by mentoring students to apply for Chevening and other UK scholarships, so every cohort strengthens the ecosystem that produced me.

The network also strengthens my teaching: connecting my campus to UK universities through guest lectures and research collaboration, and to industry alumni who bring real projects into the classroom. What I contribute is a bridge between Indonesian and German business cultures — a bridge Chevening scholars can keep crossing long after I graduate.
